\begin{abstract}
The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) Mission Need requires $\sqrt{s}$ = 20–100 GeV (upgradable to 140 GeV) and luminosity 10$^{33}$–10$^{34}$ cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$. The current ring-ring baseline achieves the full scope, including $\sim$10$^{34}$ cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$ across all energies. However, when the design is re-optimized for the lower boundary — accepting ~10$^{33}$ cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$ and prioritizing cost — an alternative configuration emerges as more advantageous: a hybrid LHeC-like electron accelerator using multi-pass energy recovery linacs (ERL).
This solution reduces electron-beam power by roughly an order of magnitude, yielding nearly a factor of two reduction in total project cost compared with the present baseline while still satisfying the minimum physics requirements. The study performs parametric cost and performance modeling, augmented by AI-driven optimization, to explore this design space.
Serving primarily as an educational exercise for the next generation of accelerator physicists and engineers, the paper demonstrates modern design methods: rapid parametric scans, cost-driven optimization, and integration of AI tools. It examines technical feasibility, identifies critical R\&D (high-current ERL operation, beam–beam effects, synchronization, etc.), and discusses how such re-optimization studies can be used to train designers in an era when artificial intelligence dramatically expands exploration of complex accelerator parameter spaces.
\end{abstract}

\section{Preamble -- AI and Education}

Educating future accelerator scientists in the AI era require developing new approaches. This paper is not an ultimate answer, but just a small step, hopefully in the right direction, along the path of finding optimal methodology of transferring knowledge to the new generation in the new world of unprecedented acceleration of analysis, simulations, and coding, due to proliferation of AI.    

The emergence of AI-assisted coding presents both unique challenges and unprecedented opportunities for accelerator physics education. Institutions like Fermilab, which hosts the US Particle Accelerator School , and CERN, home to the CERN Accelerator School CAS, need to rapidly adapt their pedagogical approaches to this new reality. Launch of the Genesis program in the US further encourages use of AI for accelerated design of particle accelerators. 

In this paper, we will try to mimic what can be done in one-week USPAS course on accelerator design, where students typically analyze and collectively develop a mini project. Past examples of such USPAS mini-projects are compact LWFA-fed ring light source \cite{USPAS2016} or ERL-based EUV ligh source \cite{USPAS2023}. 

Those projects mentioned above were performed without use of AI. For the present paper, we use the same approach of USPAS mini-project, but assume that students will have access to AI tools, and will make most of design steps using AI. The authors of this paper are not students, however, we are trying to imagine what we could do in one week, if we were given the described below mini-project. In a sense, the paper is a rehearsal or a draft script for a future USPAS course, where accelerator mini-projects will be evaluated and conceptualized using AI tools. 

\section{EIC-LHeC mini-project}

The EIC-LHeC mini-project is conceived in the spirit of a one-week USPAS accelerator design course, where a small team of students is given a specific design challenge and expected to produce a self-consistent conceptual study by the end of the week. Past USPAS mini-projects -- such as a compact LWFA-fed ring light source~\cite{USPAS2016} and an ERL-based EUV light source~\cite{USPAS2023} -- were completed without AI assistance. The present work uses the same pedagogical framework, but now assumes that students have access to modern AI tools throughout every stage of the design process.

The mini-project assignment can be stated as follows: \textit{Given the EIC proton beam stored in the RHIC-derived Hadron Storage Ring, design the simplest and most cost-effective electron accelerator complex that satisfies the EIC CD0 Mission Need lower boundary -- a center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s} \approx 29$--100~GeV and an average luminosity of $\mathcal{L} \sim 10^{33}$~cm$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$ -- and estimate its cost relative to the ring-ring baseline.}

The motivation for this re-examination comes from discussions at the European Strategy for Particle Physics (ESPP) Open Symposium in July~2025~\cite{ESPP}, where it was argued that inserting a new electron storage ring into the LHC tunnel for LHeC would be both cost-prohibitive and luminosity-limiting. This observation prompted the question: could a similar trade-off -- replacing an electron storage ring with a superconducting energy-recovery linac (ERL) -- be beneficial in the EIC context as well?

The proposed hybrid configuration keeps the full EIC proton beam infrastructure intact and replaces the electron storage ring with a multi-pass ERL, inspired by the LHeC linac-ring design~\cite{ESPP-LHeC-submission}. Electrons are accelerated in a superconducting RF (SRF) linac, transported through recirculation arcs, brought to collision, and then decelerated back through the linac, recovering most of the beam energy. This approach has several appealing features compared to the ring-ring baseline:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The electron beam power is reduced by roughly an order of magnitude, since each electron bunch is used only once rather than stored for millions of turns.
    \item The absence of a large electron storage ring substantially reduces civil construction and magnet costs.
    \item A 6~GeV SRF linac traversed three times reaches 18~GeV, covering the full EIC energy range without additional hardware.
    \item The electron beam emittance can in principle be kept very small, since it is set at the injector and not blown up by synchrotron radiation in a ring.
\end{itemize}

The layout of this hybrid design is shown schematically in Fig.~\ref{fig:lhec-layout}: a 6~GeV SRF ERL linac is installed across a diameter of the existing RHIC/EIC tunnel, with recirculation arcs using conventional (non-FFA) magnets routed around the outside of the ion ring. The electron beam is delivered to the interaction region, shared with the ePIC detector, and the spent beam is dumped after energy recovery.

The mini-project proceeds through the following design steps, each of which was carried out with AI assistance and is described in subsequent sections of this paper:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Parametric modeling of beam parameters, luminosity, and beam-beam effects for the baseline EIC, the reference LHeC design, and the proposed hybrid (\textit{EIC-LHeC parameters} section).
    \item Evaluation of beam-beam effects and electron disruption at the interaction point (\textit{Beam-beam evaluations} section).
    \item Conceptual IR optics design and estimation of final-doublet aperture requirements (\textit{IR optics evaluations} section).
    \item Assessment of IR magnet technology, including the choice between \NoCaseChange{Nb}--\NoCaseChange{Ti} and \NoCaseChange{Nb$_3$Sn} superconducting options (\textit{IR magnets evaluations} section).
    \item Feasibility analysis of energy recovery at high current and with a disrupted electron beam (\textit{Energy recovery evaluations} section).
    \item Design of the recirculation arc optics and transition sections (\textit{Recirculation evaluations} section).
    \item Parametric cost estimation of the electron complex, interaction region, and detector, benchmarked against the LHeC cost study~\cite{LHeC-costs}, and converted to USA DOE accounting (\textit{Cost estimations} sections).
    \item Identification of the critical R\&D items and open questions (\textit{Required R\&D and next steps} section).
\end{enumerate}

Throughout all these steps, AI tools -- primarily large language models with code generation capability -- were used to write Python scripts, interpret results, explore parameter trade-offs, and draft text. The role of the ``students'' (in this rehearsal, the authors) was to formulate physically meaningful prompts, critically verify the outputs against known accelerator physics principles, and iteratively refine both the prompts and the results. This workflow is described in detail in the \textit{Verification of AI results} section.

\subsection{Approach and AI Prompting Strategy}

The parameter development for the EIC-LHeC hybrid followed an iterative dialogue 
with AI tools, beginning from the two parent designs and converging on a 
self-consistent hybrid configuration. The starting prompt sequence was designed 
to be accessible to a student with basic accelerator physics knowledge, 
progressively building up from simple luminosity calculations to beam-beam 
constraints and cost-driven optimization.

The initial prompts requested Python codes to compute beam parameters and 
luminosity for the standard EIC and LHeC designs separately, using published 
parameter tables~\cite{EIC-pars,ESPP-LHeC-submission} as numerical inputs. 
The AI was asked to use normalized emittances, compute geometric emittances via 
the relativistic $\gamma$ factor, calculate beam sizes at the interaction point 
(IP), and output a formatted parameter table. Beam-beam tune shift parameters 
$\xi_{x,y}$ and RMS beam divergences at the IP were added in subsequent prompts, 
as these quantities constrain the design and must be monitored during optimization.

\subsection{Resolving Parameter Inconsistencies in the Hybrid}

When the AI was first asked to combine EIC proton parameters with LHeC electron 
parameters into a single hybrid collider, it correctly identified several 
fundamental inconsistencies between the two parent designs and flagged them 
before producing any code. The main conflicts were:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bunch repetition rate mismatch.} The EIC ring circumference 
    of 3834~m gives a revolution frequency of $\sim$78~kHz and, with 1160 
    bunches, a collision frequency of $\sim$91~MHz. The LHeC electron linac, 
    matched to the LHC ring, assumes $\sim$32~MHz. Naively combining the two 
    would imply an electron beam current of $\sim$1.45~A -- far beyond the 
    LHeC design value of 60~mA.
    \item \textbf{Beam size mismatch at the IP.} LHeC electron optics 
    ($\beta^*_{x,y} = 0.20$~m, $\varepsilon_{n} = 22$~\textmu m) were 
    optimized to match the tiny $\sim$10~\textmu m proton beam sizes in the 
    7~TeV LHC. EIC protons at 275~GeV have much larger emittances, 
    giving $\sigma_x \approx 173$~\textmu m and $\sigma_y \approx 16$~\textmu m 
    at the LHeC $\beta^*$ -- a severe luminosity and beam-beam penalty.
    \item \textbf{Bunch population and current limits.} Keeping the LHeC 
    electron bunch population of $N_e \approx 1.19 \times 10^{10}$ at the EIC 
    collision frequency would require $\sim$1.45~A electron current, violating 
    the LHeC design limit. Conversely, enforcing 60~mA at the EIC frequency 
    would require reducing $N_e$ to $\sim$$4 \times 10^9$.
\end{itemize}

The resolution adopted in the mini-project was to reduce the number of proton 
bunches by a factor of three relative to the EIC baseline (from 1160 to 386), 
proportionally increasing the bunch population while keeping the total proton 
beam current constant at 1.0~A. This brings the collision frequency down to 
$\sim$30~MHz, closely matching the LHeC electron bunch rate and restoring a 
physically consistent electron beam current of $\sim$58~mA. The IP optics were 
then re-optimized to match proton and electron beam sizes at the new operating 
point, yielding $\beta^*_x = 1.20$~m, $\beta^*_y = 0.05$~m for electrons and 
$\beta^*_x = 0.12$~m, $\beta^*_y = 0.06$~m for protons. This discussion with 
the AI also highlighted that the electron beam-beam parameter $\xi_e$ is 
relatively unconstrained in the linac-ring scheme, since electrons are discarded 
after a single pass; the binding constraint is the \emph{proton} beam-beam 
parameter, which was kept below $\xi_p < 0.01$.

A further optimization step explored paths to $\mathcal{L} = 10^{34}$~cm$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$, 
relying on: (i)~reduction of proton emittances via coherent electron cooling 
at collision energy; and (ii)~reduction of the vertical electron emittance 
using a magnetized electron gun followed by Derbenev's flat-beam transformer, 
conservatively assumed to reduce $\varepsilon_{n,y}^e$ by a factor of ten.

The initial hybrid achieves $\mathcal{L} \approx 2\times10^{33}$~cm$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$
with no changes to the EIC proton emittances, satisfying the CD0 lower boundary. 
The proton beam-beam parameter remains safely below 0.01 in both configurations. 
The electron beam-beam parameter substantially exceeds unity, reflecting severe 
single-pass disruption; the electrons are discarded after collision, so this 
does not pose a stability problem in itself, but it does raise questions about 
the feasibility of energy recovery for a highly disrupted bunch -- a point 
addressed in the \textit{Energy Recovery evaluations} section.

The optimized configuration reaches $10^{34}$~cm$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$ by reducing 
the proton emittances (assuming on-collision-energy electron cooling) by roughly 
a factor of four horizontally and three vertically relative to the initial hybrid, 
and by reducing the vertical electron emittance by a factor of ten via flat-beam 
transformation. The large proton divergences in the optimized case -- up to 
182~\textmu rad horizontally -- imply tight aperture requirements on the final 
focusing doublet, which are examined in the \textit{IR optics evaluations} section.

\section{Initial Cost Estimations}

\subsection{Approach and Scope}

The cost estimation for the hybrid EIC-LHeC electron complex was developed 
using AI-assisted parametric scaling from the 2018 LHeC cost study~\cite{LHeC-costs}, 
which provides a detailed breakdown of the electron linac complex at CERN. 
The scope of the estimate covers only the new electron beam complex, the 
interaction region (IR), and the detector; it explicitly excludes any upgrades 
to the existing EIC proton beam infrastructure, since the hybrid concept assumes 
the RHIC-derived Hadron Storage Ring is used without major modification.

The prompts used in this step asked the AI to: identify relevant cost line items 
from the LHeC reference; scale each item to the EIC-LHeC parameters 
(principally the lower linac energy of 5--6~GeV compared to the LHeC linac); 
include an estimate of the required person-years (PY) of labor effort; and 
compare two linac configurations -- a single-pass 10~GeV linac and a 
recirculating 5~GeV linac traversed twice to reach the same final energy. 
The AI was also asked to convert the resulting European cost estimate, given in 
MCHF, to a USA DOE cost accounting basis.

It should be noted that the cost scaling was performed with AI assistance and 
has not been independently verified in detail. The IR and detector cost 
line items were manually increased relative to the raw AI scaling output, 
as the automated scaling was judged to underestimate these components. 
The estimates should therefore be regarded as indicative and order-of-magnitude 
in nature, pending a more rigorous engineering cost review.

\subsection{Single-Pass versus Recirculating Linac}

Two configurations for the electron linac were compared:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-pass:} A 10~GeV SRF linac, through which the electron 
    beam passes once before collision and once during energy recovery deceleration. 
    This is the conceptually simplest configuration but requires the full SRF 
    installed length for 10~GeV acceleration.
    \item \textbf{Recirculating:} A 5~GeV SRF linac (in practice assumed capable 
    of 6~GeV) through which the beam passes twice on the way up and twice on the 
    way down, reaching 10~GeV (or up to 18~GeV with three passes) with half the 
    SRF installed length. The additional cost is two recirculation arcs with 
    conventional (non-FFA) magnets and associated transport optics.
\end{itemize}

The recirculating configuration is directly analogous to the LHeC linac-ring 
design and to the operating principle of CEBAF at Jefferson Lab. It reduces 
the dominant SRF cost roughly by half while adding a comparatively modest 
arc cost, yielding a lower total. A 6~GeV linac with three recirculations 
would cover the full EIC energy range up to 18~GeV, providing useful 
headroom above the 10~GeV nominal operating point.

\section{Required R\&D and Next Steps}

The hybrid EIC-LHeC concept rests on several assumptions that require 
dedicated R\&D before the design can be considered credible at project-proposal 
level. The principal open questions, identified in dialogue with AI, are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High-current ERL operation.} Energy recovery efficiency 
    of $\lesssim 10^{-4}$ fractional loss is required at 58~mA and 10~GeV. 
    A staged program -- low-current/high-energy at CEBAF, high-current/low-energy 
    at PERLE -- would bound the two critical parameters.
    \item \textbf{Polarized electron source current.} The 58~mA design 
    value far exceeds the present state of the art ($\sim$1~mA CW). 
    Multiplexing $N$ sources via RF beam mergers is a plausible path once 
    10~mA from a single source is demonstrated at Jefferson Lab.
    \item \textbf{IR final doublet and collimation.} The small proton 
    $\beta^*$ (0.09--0.12~m) drives large IP divergences ($\sim$300~\textmu rad), 
    requiring large-aperture SC quadrupoles and tight collimation, with 
    potential impact on 4$\pi$ detector coverage and MDI compatibility 
    with ePIC.
    \item \textbf{Intrabeam scattering.} Higher proton bunch charge 
    (factor-of-three increase) raises the IBS rate; electron cooling must 
    overcome IBS growth to maintain the emittances assumed in the 
    $10^{34}$ optimized configuration.
    \item \textbf{Energy recovery of disrupted beam.} Electron 
    beam-beam parameters $\xi_y^e \approx 6.8$ imply severe single-pass 
    disruption. Whether the resulting phase-space volume fits within 
    return-arc and ERL deceleration acceptance requires dedicated 
    simulation.
    \item \textbf{ePIC detector compatibility.} Any change to the proton 
    FD optics or electron beam delivery must preserve the existing MDI 
    geometry, solenoid compensation, and synchrotron radiation masking. 
    Early engagement with the ePIC detector group is essential.
\end{enumerate}

Logical next steps are: (1)~systematic beam-beam parameter scans with 
strong-strong or weak-strong codes; (2)~conceptual IR lattice and MDI 
assessment; (3)~ERL lattice design and disrupted-beam acceptance study; 
(4)~IBS/cooling power modeling; (5)~bottom-up cost re-estimation; and 
(6)~programmatic compatibility assessment with the EIC CD schedule. 
The hybrid design illustrates how AI-assisted parametric exploration can 
rapidly map an alternative design space and surface the critical questions 
for a more detailed study -- accelerating the early conceptual phase 
without replacing rigorous engineering analysis.
